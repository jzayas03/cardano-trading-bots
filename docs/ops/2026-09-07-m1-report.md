# M1 report — the first long collector run

Written 2026-09-07, from the run that started 02:20 UTC that day.

## Verdict

**M1 asked for 24 continuous hours of collection. That was not met.** The run
covered 20.8 hours and produced 108 ticks where a 600-second interval implies
125, so 17 ticks are missing from inside the window as well.

The run is still worth keeping. It produced four findings. Two are fixed and
merged, one is a budget conclusion that stands, and one is a trap in our own
schema that I fell into while writing this report and corrected before filing it.

**The clean 24 hours has not been run.** It should be run on the configuration
described under "What changed", and M1 should be declared met from that run,
not this one.

## Finding 1 — the request budget does not fit

Blockfrost's own usage meter, which is the authority for the quota:

| Day (UTC) | Requests | Share of the 50,000 free tier |
|---|---|---|
| 2026-09-06 (complete day) | 49,224 | 98% |
| 2026-09-07 (to 23:10) | 48,088 | 96% |

Two days within 2% of the ceiling, with three collector restarts between them.
There is no headroom for a restart, and a restart is the one thing that happens
whenever the machine sleeps or the code changes.

The cost is structural, not incidental. A refresh tick spends 297 requests to
read 20 pools, so 14.9 per pool. A full day at 600 seconds is 144 ticks:

```
144 x 297  =  42,768   refresh
           +   5,692   one discovery sweep
           =  48,460   =  97% of the free tier
```

Nothing was rejected. There are no 402 or 429 responses in the collector log.
The single "402" a plain grep finds is four digits inside a millisecond
timestamp, not a status code.

## Finding 2 — `discovery_calls` is a breakdown of `provider_calls`, not an addition to it

Our own counter agrees with the vendor's meter to within half a percent:

| Source | Requests, 2026-09-07 UTC to 23:10 |
|---|---|
| Blockfrost's meter | 48,088 |
| Our `provider_calls` | 47,893 |

I reported a 33% gap between these two earlier in the evening. That gap was an
artefact of my own query, not a property of the system. On a tick that
discovers, `provider_calls` already contains every discovery request, and
`discovery_calls` is the per-venue *breakdown* of that same number. Every
discovery tick confirms it exactly:

| Tick | `provider_calls` | sum of `discovery_calls` |
|---|---|---|
| 70 | 5,692 | 5,692 |
| 62 | 2,405 | 2,405 |
| 53 | 2,405 | 2,405 |
| 52 | 5,691 | 5,691 |

Adding the two columns double-counts every discovery sweep. `npm run doctor`
has always summed it correctly, which is why its projection tracked the vendor
meter while my ad-hoc SQL did not.

**Consequence:** `provider_calls` is trustworthy, the budget arithmetic above
stands, and any new budget query must sum `provider_calls` alone. Reconcile
against the vendor meter anyway, since it is the authority and costs one
request:

```
GET https://cardano-mainnet.blockfrost.io/api/v0/metrics
header: project_id: <key>
```

It returns per-UTC-day counts for the last 30 days and lags the live counter,
so treat its figure for the current day as a floor.

## Finding 3 — a suspended laptop stamped data as older than it was

The collector pinned the tick boundary before sleeping, so when the machine
suspended and woke, the row carried the pre-sleep label while holding
post-wake observations.

Thirty-three of 108 ticks were stamped more than two minutes before the
observation was taken, the worst by 15 minutes. Data therefore looked *fresher*
than it was to anything that trusts the label, including the stale-fill bound
the paper engine uses.

Fixed in PR #55. The boundary is now reconciled against the wall clock at the
moment the tick actually runs, inside `runTick`, so every caller is covered
rather than only the CLI loop.

## Finding 4 — a venue outage splices a token's series across pools

Sixteen of 20 tokens drew candles from more than one pool inside 24 hours.
When the deepest pool's venue fails to answer, the collector writes the next
deepest, and the resulting series silently changes its underlying market.

The policy decision was to keep deepest-per-tick and make the splice visible
rather than to pin a pool. A run now declares in its warnings when its candle
series spans more than one pool (PR #52).

## What changed, and what it should do

`COLLECT_MIN_DEPTH_ADA=400000` refreshes only tokens whose deepest pool holds
at least 400,000 ADA. Measured against current depth it keeps **17 of 20**
tokens and drops three:

| Dropped | Pool depth |
|---|---|
| AGIX | 130,373 ADA |
| COPI | 37,685 ADA |
| SONG | 9 ADA |

SONG at 9 ADA was never a tradeable market, so this is a data-quality
improvement as much as a budget one.

**It is not enough on its own.** Refresh cost scales with the number of tokens
refreshed, so dropping 3 of 20 takes a tick from 297 requests to about 252:

| Floor | Tokens kept | Projected day | Share of tier |
|---|---|---|---|
| off (today) | 20 | 48,460 | 97% |
| 400,000 ADA | 17 | 42,023 | 84% |
| 800,000 ADA | 13 | 33,487 | 67% |

At 84% a single extra restart still overruns the cap. Two options create real
margin, and the choice is yours:

- Raise the floor to **800,000 ADA**, which keeps 13 tokens (through IAG at
  834,789 ADA) and leaves room for two restarts.
- Keep 17 tokens and raise the interval above 600 seconds.

These are projections from a measured per-tick cost, not measurements. Run one
full day on the new configuration and read the vendor meter at the end.

## What this report does not claim

- That 24 hours of continuous collection has been demonstrated. It has not.
- That the tick-label fix works in production. It is merged and unit-tested;
  the collector had not yet restarted onto it when this was written.
- That the depth floor reduces traffic by any particular amount. The 84% and
  67% figures are projections from a measured per-tick cost, not measurements.
- That `npm run test:live` passes on axios 0.33 against Blockfrost. Still never
  run.
